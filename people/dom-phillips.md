It states that he was 57, that he had reported for The Guardian, the Financial Times, The Washington Post and The New York Times, and that he had covered illegal mining, deforestation and human rights issues in the Amazon. The page title is "The Cerrado: how Brazil's vital 'water tank' went from forest to soy fields". The byline is Dom Phillips, linked to his Guardian profile, <https://www.theguardian.com/profile/dom-phillips>, which lists this article. The page's `article:published_time` is `2020-11-25T15:00:31.000Z`, and its canonical address is the address above. It appears in the Guardian's "Animals farmed" series. An existing Internet Archive capture from 26 November 2020, <https://web.archive.org/web/20201126041756/https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2020/nov/25/the-cerrado-how-brazils-vital-water-tank-went-from-forest-to-soy-fields>, was checked on 26 October 2026. It retains the title, the byline, the same publication time and the article text.

The article describes how, within a few decades, farming turned the Cerrado, the tropical savannah across central Brazil, into a centre of soya and beef production. It gives MapBiomas's figure that 44% of the Cerrado's native vegetation is already farmed, reports 6,500 square kilometres cleared in 2019 and more than 61,000 fire alerts in 2020 to that date, and quotes a researcher who calls the region Brazil's water tank. It traces the region's development from the move of the capital to Brasília in 1960, through military-era programmes and a Japanese-Brazilian scheme of the late 1970s, to the export trade. It contrasts the protection given to the Amazon with the much smaller share of protected land in the Cerrado. It gives voice to a farmer who arrived in 1997 and to a member of the Melancias community in Piauí, which is in a legal dispute over its land. It ends by setting record soya harvests and their importance to Brazil's economy during the pandemic against the long-term loss of forest and wildlife.
